## In Depth

### Tool Calling in Spring AI 2.0: A Composable, Agentic Architecture

Spring AI 2.0 fundamentally reimagines how AI models interact with application functions, elevating tool calling from buried implementation detail to first-class architectural component. The core innovation is lifting the tool execution loop into the advisor chain, making it composable, observable, and extensible in ways the 1.x architecture never allowed.

Defining tools is remarkably simple. Annotate any method with @Tool, add parameter descriptions via @ToolParam, and Spring AI automatically generates the JSON schema. Pass your tool-containing objects to ChatClient via .tools(), and you're done. Under the hood, the ToolCallingAdvisor orchestrates everything: it injects tool definitions into context, sends conversation history to the LLM, inspects responses for tool calls, executes those tools, and loops until the model produces a final answer—all while supporting both blocking and streaming modes.

What makes this architecture powerful is composability. Where you place other advisors relative to ToolCallingAdvisor (default order HIGHEST_PRECEDENCE + 300) determines their scope. Place MessageChatMemoryAdvisor outside the loop (order 200), and it captures only initial questions and final answers—keeping your memory store lean and compatible with every repository implementation. Place it inside, and every tool request and response gets persisted, useful for debugging but potentially overwhelming for production memory systems.

This recursive advisor pattern—where an advisor can re-enter the downstream chain repeatedly—isn't just for tool calling. It powers structured-output retry loops, evaluation loops, and any scenario where you need iterative refinement. Spring AI 2.0 doesn't just add agentic capabilities; it provides the primitives to build arbitrarily sophisticated AI workflows while maintaining clean separation of concerns. For developers building on Spring Boot, this represents a massive leap forward in creating AI systems that can discover information, take action, and autonomously work toward goals.

*Source: [Spring Blog](https://spring.io/blog/2026/06/15/spring-ai-composable-tool-calling)*

### The White House Is Making Up Its Rules for AI in Real Time

Nearly a week after the Trump administration forced Anthropic to pull Claude Mythos and Fable 5 offline via export control directive, the standoff continues—and it reveals just how improvisational AI regulation has become under this administration. Anthropic maintains it followed all established procedures and coordinated with the government on Mythos rollout. The White House counters that the company acted recklessly and can't be trusted with frontier models. The problem? Neither side is citing concrete, published rules.

The core dispute centers on two issues. First, US officials grew alarmed when they learned Anthropic shared Mythos with SK Telecom, a South Korean telecom they allege has China ties—despite Anthropic having worked with SK Telecom for years without prior objection and immediately revoking access when concerns were raised. Second, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy reported to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent that some Fable 5 guardrails could be circumvented, though the severity and exploitability of these bypasses remain unclear.

What's striking is the opacity and inconsistency. The White House has never clearly articulated what Anthropic did wrong—the best public statement is a vague post from tech adviser David Sacks. No formal rules were violated because few formal rules exist; the Trump administration has aggressively blocked AI regulation efforts, arguing they hamper innovation and help China. Yet when a company crosses an undefined line, the response is swift and severe.

The irony is profound: in trying to protect American AI leadership, the administration has locked Anthropic's own researchers out of their most advanced models, preventing the very innovation the policy supposedly defends. Apple, Meta, and hundreds of enterprise customers lost access overnight. We're now in an era where AI companies face serious consequences for violating norms that were never written down, negotiated behind closed doors with officials who change positions unpredictably. For anyone building on Claude or other frontier models, this regulatory uncertainty is arguably more damaging than any clear rulebook would be.

*Source: [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/anthropic-mythos-export-controls-ai-regulations/)*

### Try One of macOS 27's Best Features Right Now

Apple announced at WWDC that macOS 27 will let you build Apple Shortcuts using generative AI—just describe what you want in plain language, and the system builds it. For anyone who's wrestled with Shortcuts' visual programming interface, this is transformative. But you don't need to wait until autumn. Federico Viticci of MacStories couldn't wait either, so he built Shortcuts Playground, which brings AI-powered shortcut generation to Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex today.

Getting started requires installing the Shortcuts Playground agent via a Terminal command (instructions on GitHub). Once installed, type /shortcuts in Claude Code to trigger it. You'll see options including shortcuts-playground:build for creating new shortcuts from scratch and shortcuts-playground:remix for modifying existing ones. Describe what you want—say, a shortcut that compiles today's weather, calendar appointments, and to-do list, then reads everything aloud—and the agent gets to work.

The agent doesn't just blindly generate code. It asks clarifying questions when your requirements are ambiguous and explains what is and isn't possible within Shortcuts' constraints. While AI-generated shortcuts might not be perfect on the first try (let's be honest, AI-built things rarely are), they provide a solid foundation you can refine. This dramatically lowers the barrier to automation, especially for complex workflows that would take significant time to construct manually.

For developers interested in Claude Code's capabilities beyond chat, Shortcuts Playground demonstrates the platform's potential as a development assistant. It's particularly valuable for macOS power users who want to automate repetitive tasks but find Shortcuts' interface intimidating. The fact that this exists months before Apple's official feature shows both the power of current AI coding assistants and the enthusiasm in the developer community for practical automation tools.

*Source: [Wired](https://www.wired.com/story/try-one-of-macos-27s-best-features-right-now-shortcuts-playground-agent/)*

### Jakarta NoSQL: Why JPA Is Not Enough for the AI Era

AI has fundamentally transformed enterprise persistence requirements, pushing architectures beyond the relational-only world that made JPA a Jakarta EE cornerstone. Modern AI applications demand document stores for flexible schemas, caches for low-latency serving, graph databases for relationship mapping, and increasingly, vector databases for semantic search and retrieval-augmented generation. Jakarta NoSQL addresses this polyglot persistence reality by providing a unified programming model across diverse database types. The article makes the case that JPA's relational assumptions are too rigid for AI workloads and that Jakarta EE needs native NoSQL support to remain relevant.

*[DZone Java](https://dzone.com/articles/jakarta-nosql-jpa-ai-era)*

### MongoDB-backed Spring Batch jobs and more in Spring Boot 4.1

Spring Batch's long-standing SQL database requirement has finally been eliminated in Spring Boot 4.1. Originally designed years before MongoDB existed, Spring Batch maintained meticulous job state in a JobRepository that spoke only SQL dialect—frustrating for teams running MongoDB-centric architectures. The update enables MongoDB-backed job repositories, letting Spring Batch store job execution metadata, step contexts, and execution history directly in MongoDB collections. This removes a significant architectural friction point and aligns Spring Batch with modern polyglot persistence strategies, particularly important as more teams adopt document stores for AI and event-driven workloads.

*[Spring Blog](https://spring.io/blog/2026/06/21/spring-boot-41-and-spring-batch)*
